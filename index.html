DesignWhat the board does

It's a thin, bendable board that sits on the arm, reads muscle signals (EMG) through the electrode pads, and streams the data over Bluetooth to a phone app. I did the schematic and the 2-layer flex layout in Altium.

  • Separate 3.3 V analog (AVDD) and 1.8 V digital (DVDD) supplies, each from its own LDO, so digital noise stays out of the sensor signal
  • A 16-bit differential ADC to pick up the small EMG voltages
  • A BLE microcontroller on a tab at the end of the strip
  • Wavy (serpentine) traces near the chips so the copper can flex without cracking

AssemblySoldering a chip onto a flexible board

Flex boards are harder to solder than normal ones because they bend and can warp under heat. I hand-assembled and reworked 3 prototypes under a stereo microscope:

  • Put flux on the fine-pitch pads and checked the coverage under the scope
  • Placed the BGA chip with tweezers, then reflowed it with a hot-air gun moving in slow circles
  • As the solder melts, surface tension pulls the chip onto its pads by itself, so placement only has to be close

I also wrote up the whole process as a step-by-step guide so the rest of the team could assemble boards too.

Digital designHow it works

The chip reads pairs of 64-bit numbers out of SRAM, adds them with a 32-bit adder in two passes, and writes the 64-bit result back. I took it all the way from RTL to a routed layout.

  • Controller: a 7-state FSM (IDLE, READ, READ2, ADD, ADD2, WRITE, END) that walks through memory with read and write pointers
  • Adder: 32-bit ripple carry built from full adders in a generate loop. The low half goes first and its carry feeds the high half
  • Result buffer: holds the low and high halves until the full 64 bits are ready to write
  • Memory: two CF_SRAM_1024x32 macros, one for each 32-bit half

One bug I hit: in the ADD state I had to use the SRAM output r_data[31:0] directly, because the register I meant to use wasn't updated yet that cycle. It made me a lot more careful about which cycle a value is actually valid in.

VerificationTestbench and coverage

I built a UVM-style testbench from scratch: sequence item, sequencer, driver, monitor, and scoreboard, all connected to the design through an interface. The scoreboard checks every result the chip writes back against the expected sum.

I also wrote assertions for the things that are easy to break, like the carry making it from the low half to the high half, the halves being written in the right order, and reset behavior.

In Cadence IMC, FSM and toggle coverage both came out at 100%. One code block never gets hit, which is why the overall number is 99.82%.

Physical designGetting to zero DRCs

I ran the full RTL-to-GDS flow in Innovus: floorplan, placement, clock tree synthesis, and routing. The design I started with had over 1,000 DRC violations.

I went through the SRAM LEF obstructions to figure out where routing wasn't allowed around the macros, then fixed it step by step:

  • 15-unit placement halos around the SRAMs
  • routing blockages on MET1 to MET3 over the macros
  • opening up more metal layers for routing

That got it to zero. Setup timing went from −2.709 ns to +0.01 ns and hold from −0.032 ns to 0.

I also wrote a Python script that reads the timing report, counts the failing paths (50), and finds the average gate depth (about 6). Plus some Tcl procs to highlight the SRAMs and critical paths in the GUI.

Cars bounce all the time, and shock absorbers just turn that motion into heat. I wanted to see if an EV could get some of that energy back as electricity, so I designed a suspension system to do it.

How it works

  • A hydraulic bypass with check valves sends fluid one way through a vane motor, so it spins whether the shock is compressing or extending
  • The vane motor drives a generator that charges the battery
  • I ran calculations and simulations on the energy conversion and estimated about 6.8% more driving range

The app

I also built a Bluetooth app to monitor and control the system in real time: vehicle height, suspension settings, and a "GreenPower" mode that turns regeneration on. The app interface is copyrighted.
